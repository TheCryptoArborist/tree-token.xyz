    These names describe model position. A band alone does not tell you when to buy or sell.

    How to read this chart

    Follow Bitcoin's price through the colored zones, then compare it with the model curve for the same date.

    1. Check the date

      Observations use UTC daily closes. The chart's data date can lag the price trading now.

    2. Read proportional moves

      The vertical axis is logarithmic: a doubling takes the same distance at any price level.

    3. Compare with the trend

      This version fits price against Bitcoin's age and adjusts band widths for each halving cycle's volatility.

    4. Use the wider context

      Price can stay in a zone for months. The fitted model can change; a band does not predict a reversal.

    Use this model's own labels. Other Rainbow charts use different formulas and can place the same price in a different band.

    Understanding the provider's current reading

    The provider displays these six values together for one observation date. These are field explanations; the latest figures are available on the source page.

    BTC price
    Bitcoin's observed daily close in US dollars.
    Model price
    The fitted trend value on that date. It is a reference, not a price target.
    Vs model
    The percentage above or below the trend: (BTC price ÷ model price − 1) × 100.
    Band floor
    The lower boundary of the occupied band. A model boundary is not guaranteed support.
    Band
    The named zone and its position from 1 (lowest) to 9 (highest).
    Band ceiling
    The upper boundary of the occupied band. An outer boundary may be open-ended.

    “Open-ended” means the model has no boundary on that side; it does not mean $0. Compare values from the same source date.

    Research notes & verification

    Before treating a historical band as a signal, identify the precise model version, its fitted data at that time, and the UTC price series being compared. A revised chart fitted afterward is a hindsight reconstruction.

    • Price provenance: The embedded provider displays its own historical price observations and update date. We do not override them with a separately fetched quote.
    • Model provenance: This version fits a long-term Bitcoin trend and adjusts band width for each halving cycle's observed volatility. It differs from other Rainbow formulas.
    • Historical caution: A June 2022 public “Fire Sale” observation preceded further price declines. It does not establish the exact first-entry date; an event-study backtest needs verified archived band rules.
    • Availability: If the embedded chart cannot load, its provider attribution link beneath the chart offers a direct source fallback. TREE does not replace unavailable market data with invented values.

    What goes into the CBBI score?

    Each card explains one ingredient and shows its CBBI score out of 100. Higher scores mean that measure looks hotter relative to past cycles. CBBI adjusts the original measurements to put these scores on the same scale.

    How are the scores combined, and what can they miss?

    The provider adjusts each indicator against historical cycle patterns, then averages the available component scores. The large number above is the provider’s published overall score. Individual dates can differ, so an older card is labeled.

    A score of 0 or 100 can persist when a measure reaches the model’s lower or upper limit. Indicators can disagree, and earlier scores may change when the provider revises its model. Past cycle patterns do not guarantee the next top or bottom.

    Bitcoin: price versus estimated holder cost

    “On-chain” means recorded on Bitcoin’s blockchain. These charts use the price when coins last moved to estimate their holders’ average cost. Start with the dollar amount, then explore the ratio.

    Realized Price: estimated cost in dollars

    Each amount of BTC is valued at the market price when it last moved, then those values are averaged across the supply. This estimates holder cost; it cannot show exactly what every owner paid.

    How to read it: Compare Bitcoin’s market-price line with the realized-price line on the same date. The gap shows how far market price is above or below the estimated cost.

    Chart by Axel Adler Jr. · Use the chart legend and provider’s UTC observation date

    Reading the two price lines

    Market price above realized price
    Bitcoin trades above the estimated cost of the supply. A wider gap means a larger premium to that estimate.
    The two prices meet
    Market price equals the estimated cost. This is the same point as an MVRV ratio of 1.
    Market price below realized price
    Bitcoin trades below the estimated cost of the supply. It can stay below that estimate or fall further.

    Reading the ratio

    Below 1
    Market price is below estimated cost. For example, 0.8 means the price is 20% below that estimate.
    At 1
    Market price and estimated cost are equal. This is the comparison point for the ratio.
    Above 1
    Market price is above estimated cost. For example, 1.5 means the price is 50% above that estimate.
    Example using made-up prices

    If Bitcoin traded at $60,000 and realized price were $40,000, MVRV would be 1.5 ($60,000 ÷ $40,000). Market price would be 50% above estimated cost.

    Why use these charts? They put Bitcoin’s price moves in the context of estimated holder costs over time. Realized Price shows that cost in dollars; MVRV shows the comparison as a ratio. Both describe the same relationship.

    Terms, calculations and what the estimate can miss
    Market price (also called spot price)
    The price one bitcoin trades for on the date you are viewing.
    Log scale
    Equal vertical steps represent equal percentage changes. A rise from $10,000 to $20,000 takes the same chart height as $20,000 to $40,000: both double the price.
    Cost basis
    The cost used to compare a holding’s value with what was paid for it. These charts estimate that cost from blockchain movements.
    Realized value (realized capitalization)
    The total value of the Bitcoin supply using the market price when each coin last moved. Divide it by the supply to get realized price.
    MVRV (Market Value to Realized Value)
    Market capitalization divided by realized capitalization. Using the same date and supply, it also equals Bitcoin’s market price divided by realized price.

    Moving coins between your own wallets can change the estimate without a purchase. Dormant or lost coins may retain very old reference prices. These are estimates across the coin supply, so they do not establish an individual holder’s profit or loss.

    Definitions: Glassnode: Realized Price and MVRV · Coin Metrics: realized capitalization.

    Price can stay above or below the estimate for long periods. Neither chart guarantees a price floor, a market top or the timing of a reversal. Read the daily observations in the context of the wider price trend.
